# Values and Design Principles

These are the morals of the product. Every feature, screen, and line of code should be checked against them. When two options are both reasonable, pick the one that fits these better.

## 1. Relieve stress, never add to it

The app exists to make people feel calmer and more in control. If a feature creates pressure, guilt, or noise, it is wrong, even if it is clever.

- Show urgency only when something is genuinely due. A missed item that is not due yet is a small marker, not an alarm.
- Never overload a day. If the plan does not fit, say so early and offer something to push back.
- Prefer one calm, clear screen over many busy ones.

## 2. One thing at a time

People get overwhelmed by long lists. The app's job is to turn a long list into the next step.

- The daily plan is chronological and focused on now.
- The full backlog exists, but the user should rarely need to stare at it.

## 3. The app does the work, the user stays in charge

Planning should feel like reviewing a good draft, not filling a blank page. But the user always has the final say.

- Automatically draft daily and weekly plans; always let the user edit, move, or remove anything.
- Explain why something was scheduled where it is, when it is not obvious.
- Never hide or delete a user's item without them knowing.

## 4. Forgiveness over perfection

Missing a day is normal. The app should help people get back on track, not punish them.

- Habits use weekly targets and consistency scores, not all-or-nothing streaks.
- Missed tasks roll into a reserve and get rescheduled, with no shame language.
- Copy should be kind and encouraging, never scolding.

## 5. Intention with minimal friction

Capturing a thought or a promise should be as fast as having it.

- One tap to capture, details later.
- Fuzzy due dates (today, tomorrow, this week, next 2 weeks, this month) instead of forcing exact dates.
- Check-ins are three taps at most.

## 6. Turn thoughts and feelings into action

Feelings are information. The journal helps people notice what they want and need, then gives them a concrete next step, so nothing stays stuck as a thought.

## 7. Earned rest is part of the plan

Free time without guilt is the real reward. The app shows open space ahead and celebrates progress, so users can relax because they know they are on track. Rest is only scheduled as an item when there is a real reason for it, so it never adds clutter.

## 8. Privacy and trust come first

Journal entries and personal plans are some of the most private things a person writes.

- Keep personal data on the device by default, and keep anything that syncs encrypted.
- Do not send journal content to outside services, including AI services, unless the user explicitly opts in.
- The app supports wellbeing but is not a mental-health service. If that boundary matters for a feature, say so clearly in the app.

## 9. Smart rules before AI

The app should feel like it understands the user through thoughtful design and well-grounded psychology, not by handing their thoughts to an AI model. Prefer clear, explainable rules. AI is only considered later, as an opt-in, if rules genuinely cannot do the job.

## 10. Built on evidence

Design decisions should trace back to user research (interviews with organizers and aspiring organizers) and to psychology research on stress, planning, habits, and motivation. When a decision is a guess, label it as one.
